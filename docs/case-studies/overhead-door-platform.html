If you run an Overhead Door distributorship, you know the shape of the problem. The phone rings all day and nobody can say which calls the website brought in. Forms arrive as emails and disappear into inboxes. The national brand does the hard part: it makes people want an Overhead Door. Turning that interest into a call to your office is the website's job, and most of the time nobody can say how well it is doing it. It lists what you sell, but it cannot tell you what it sold.

Overhead Door Company of Baltimore, in business since 1947 with 28 trucks, 11 counties and about 11,000 service calls a year, asked us to fix that. We had built and looked after their previous site for years. This time the brief was not a new look. It was a website that works like part of the business: one that sells to the commercial buyer, stays the first call for the homeowner, owns the territory in search, and shows the owner where every lead came from.

What the owner gets

Built for the commercial buyer

About seventy percent of this distributor's revenue is commercial bid work, and the inquiry history said the same: dock doors, rolling steel, drawings and specs, and nearly none of it served by the old site. So the commercial lines lead. Loading dock equipment, rolling steel, sectional steel, high-speed, security grilles and counter doors, aluminum full-view, fire-rated doors and commercial operators each have a page of their own, written in the plain register of someone who sells and services them, with the full line from the corporate catalog: models, fact strips from the spec sheets, and the documents a bid needs, on the company's own domain.

The Architects and Resources section, which the owner asked for by name, holds about seven hundred specification sheets, drawings, submittals and fire door documents, each on its own page where an estimator can find it from a search and send the link. Corporate's links are checked automatically, so a moved file never leaves a dead end. When the owner and his technical lead reviewed the approach, their note was that it "keeps the customer on our site" instead of sending the bid to the national catalog and hoping it comes back.

Then the commercial front door. "Send us your specs" takes a project name, a bid due date, a link to plans and the package itself, and goes straight to the estimators. The fire door drop test page, the one page on the old site that was already bringing in work, kept its address and its wording to the letter, with the annual inspection commitments the client wrote themselves. A facility manager with a door down at six in the morning gets the client's own same-day repair promise and a form that reaches the service desk.

Still the first call for homeowners

Commercial leads do not mean residential waits. The three garage door families, traditional steel, carriage house and insulated Thermacore, each get a page in the shape a homeowner shops: the models side by side, the questions they ask (insulation, windows, steel gauge, whether to replace the opener with the door), the options, and a free in-home estimate as the next step. The corporate door designer is linked for picking a look, with the estimate coming back to Baltimore. An opener error code, a spring that let go on a Saturday or a house outside the service counties each has a path, and the phone number is one tap away on every screen.

Owning the territory in search

The old site sent most visitors to the home page and then to a few catalog hubs, which is why nobody could tell what a visitor wanted. The new site is built the way customers search. Each product line owns one group of search terms drawn from the company's own inquiry data: customers type "roll up door" and pick "Rolling Steel" from menus, so the page targets the one and is labeled the other. Service-area pages pair each kind of work, commercial door repair, rolling steel, dock equipment, fire door testing, garage door and spring repair, with the counties served, opened county by county as the search data earns them, so a search for commercial door repair in a county lands on a page about that work in that county. Every old link forwards to its new page, and the domain itself, twenty-five years of ohdbalt.com, never changed.

The pages are also fast, which Google rewards and customers feel: in its first day live the home page scored 99 on desktop and 92 to 98 on mobile in Google PageSpeed, and every page has to keep its speed before any change goes live. Search is then worked monthly, one or two pages at a time, against the actual search terms Google reports, so the territory keeps widening after launch instead of settling.

Every lead, counted and kept

This is the part the leadership team had never had. A form on the new site is saved before anyone is notified, so a lead can no longer vanish because an email failed. Each one goes out as a single, phone-readable message to the inboxes the company's own routing rules name, with job applications going their own way. The company changes those rules itself.

Phone calls from the tracked number land in the same record, imported every night with the recording, a transcript and a three-line summary, and so do the assistant's conversations. Every lead carries the same facts: commercial, residential, service or parts; which form, chat or call it came by; and the page and the search that started the visit. For the first time the owner can see that the rolling steel page produced the bid request and the county service page produced the service call. The team still works leads from email, the way they always have; the record is for seeing, searching and exporting.

The numbers were in place before the switch. Google Analytics and Search Console went on the old site two weeks ahead of launch so there would be something honest to compare against. The monthly report is built from data the company holds, counts phone taps by product line and forms by form, and says plainly what it covers and what it does not.

An assistant that types, and never talks

At nine at night a property manager with a rolling steel door stuck half open does not want a brochure. Bolt, the site's assistant, understands what the visitor needs, answers from the company's approved content, takes down the lead and hands it to a person on the right trigger: emergency, commercial, residential, service, parts, or a job outside the territory. It is built on Anthropic's Claude, and it is deliberately limited. It never quotes a price, promises a date, interprets a warranty or confirms availability. It never places a call and never sends an email. Every line it can say beyond the approved content sits in a script the client read and approved, line by line, and its next version will be written with the company's own phone staff, in their words.

Reviews and the Google profile

The Google Business Profile had been unmanaged, a 3.7 rating on about sixty reviews with ownership unclear. Three moves: claim it in the owner's name; show live Google reviews on the home page and build a reviews page from about 180 of the company's own customer surveys, published with consent, so the proof lives on the domain they own; and ask every customer, with no filtering, after each completed job, through a short link (ohdbalt.com/review) that opens Google's review box directly, with a drafted starting point the customer can edit or discard. It starts as a weekly list the office runs and becomes automatic once the rhythm is proven.

Nothing stopped

The plan was ninety days, with the design approved by day thirty. The site went live on day sixty-nine. Phones, email and the advertising already running were never touched. The client reviewed the live site through personal links, left notes pinned to the exact spot on the page, and approved copy and the assistant's script in threads we answered one by one. Controlled test submissions ran through the real routing with the client confirming each inbox. The new site ran beside the old one for eight weeks before the switch. Launch was one switch, with the old site kept ready behind it, and a person reads the numbers every day for two weeks after.

Why this makes sense for an Overhead Door distributor

The brand already sets the standard. Corporate's catalog, documents and design language are the strongest asset a distributor has online. This build is the Baltimore edition of that standard, crimson, ribbon and all, not a departure from it, and the same edition can be made for any territory.

Commercial buyers need their own front door. Whatever your mix, the commercial buyer shops differently from the homeowner. Estimators, architects and facility managers place the larger orders, and they need specs, drawings and a direct line, not a photo gallery.

Your customers arrive by phone, by form and by chat, and they are one customer. A website that only forwards emails cannot tell you which pages, counties or product lines are producing work. One record for every channel can, and it is the beginning of knowing what your marketing is worth.

Own it. A website, a tracking number, a Google profile and the analytics behind them are business records. They belong in your name, not in an account you would lose if you changed vendors. Everything in this build is in the distributor's name, with full export, from the first day.
